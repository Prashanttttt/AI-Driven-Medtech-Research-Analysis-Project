# CRT and ICD in HFrEF: a European value story

*A one-page summary built from the re-analysis and public sources. Every claim links to its source; see [EUROPEAN_ACCESS.md](EUROPEAN_ACCESS.md) for detail.*

**Core message:** for patients with HFrEF and LVEF ≤35%, CRT and ICD reduce mortality by 28–42% compared with medical therapy, and are cost-effective in well-defined subgroups selected by QRS duration and LBBB.

| Element | Claim | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Unmet need** | Many patients with HFrEF remain at high risk of sudden death and hospitalisation despite medication; device use varies widely across Europe | Spain implants 171 ICDs per million vs ~300 European average, with a 2.5-fold regional range ([Spanish ICD Registry 2023](https://www.revespcardiol.org/en-spanish-implantable-cardioverter-defibri-articulo-S1885585724002779-pdf-file)) |
| **Clinical benefit** | Mortality reduced by 42% (CRT-D), 29% (ICD) and 28% (CRT-P) vs medical therapy | IPD network meta-analysis of 13 RCTs, 12,638 patients ([Woods 2015](https://researchonline.lshtm.ac.uk/id/eprint/2274449)) |
| **Hospitalisation** | In this project's re-analysis, the one robust signal is fewer hospitalisations in patients over 70: HR 0.80 (0.73–0.88), I² 0% | [results/sensitivity_checks.csv](../results/sensitivity_checks.csv) (3 studies; small evidence base) |
| **Who benefits most** | QRS ≥150 ms and LBBB for CRT; women gain more from CRT | [Woods 2015](https://researchonline.lshtm.ac.uk/id/eprint/2274449) |
| **Economic case** | At £30,000/QALY, CRT-D is cost-effective in 10 of 24 subgroups, including all LBBB patients in NYHA I–III | UK NHS model, lifetime horizon ([Mealing 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/100095/)) |
| **Payer alignment** | Eligibility in England, France and Germany matches the evidence: LVEF ≤35%, with QRS and LBBB defining device choice | [NICE TA314](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ta314/chapter/1-Guidance), [HAS 2024 opinion](https://www.has-sante.fr/upload/docs/evamed/CNEDIMTS-7598_QUADRA%20ALLURE%20MP_9%20juillet%202024_7598_avis.pdf), [IQTIG 2024](https://www.iqtig.org/downloads/auswertung/aj2024/hsmdef-defi-impl/DeQS-RL_HSMDEF-DEFI-IMPL_AJ2024_BUAW_V02_2024-08-15.pdf) |

## Objections a payer or HTA body may raise

| Objection | Response |
| --- | --- |
| Device costs are high | Cost-effectiveness depends on correct selection; supporting QRS/LBBB-guided device choice keeps spending where it pays back ([Mealing 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/100095/)) |
| Evidence in NYHA I and IV is thin | True: the model's results for these groups are uncertain because few trial patients were in them. Prioritise NYHA II–III |
| Benefit may be smaller now that medical therapy has improved (ARNI, SGLT2 inhibitors) | Most RCTs predate these drugs. This is a genuine evidence gap and a case for registry or post-market data |
| Pooled analyses show high heterogeneity | The IPD analysis separates CRT from ICD and models QRS, LBBB, age and sex as effect modifiers. This project's re-analysis shows how pooling everything together can mislead |

## What this project adds

The re-analysis shows that a pooled meta-analysis mixing device types, study designs and subgroups without QRS or LBBB data can produce results that contradict the RCT evidence. For a market access team, the lesson is to build value arguments on the subgroup structure payers actually use.
